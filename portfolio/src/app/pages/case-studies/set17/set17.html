Overview

This case study covers my experience working on the BoxBox Bootcamp website for Set 17. Highlights from the development and design process are featured below, as well as the feedback garnered after public release and the usage statistics over the course of the event.

CONTEXT

What is the BoxBox Bootcamp?

Bootcamp is a Riot Games-funded event run by Twitch streamer, BoxBox. It is a competition where streamers and community members try to climb the in-game ranked ladder as quickly as they can before they get eliminated from the event. Bootcamp happens at the start of every set and happens about three times a year.

Why is the Bootcamp website important?

The Bootcamp website is where participants can track how they are doing in the event. It is Bootcamp’s most critical platform as it handles eliminations and shows participant rankings in real time. It also serves as an information hub for participants and viewers alike.

THE PROBLEM

Why refactor the website?

The website was originally built using React and the codebase had been recycled for each event. Due to the quick turn-around times required for each Bootcamp, the team did not have enough time or people to consider upgrading the website. When I was brought on to the team as a dedicated designer/developer, an upgrade became feasible. The Bootcamp team wanted to completely rebuild the website using Angular, with an emphasis on modularity and future maintainability.

THE PROCESS

A tight timeline...

Our team had about 6 weeks in total to design, build the front-end from scratch, and refactor the back-end API. I worked in parallel with another developer, who handled all the back-end development, databases, and hosting infrastructure.

Focused design goals

The design for the website is based on the redesign I created for Set 15 that can be found here. I favored efficiency when updating the design since I was also responsible for developing the front-end. I focused on two main areas of improvement/change and completed the design update in about a week and a half.

01

Update the website design to match the new set theme

Swapped to space-themed backgrounds and included new set-specific assets (box borders and iconography) provided by the branding team at Riot.

Updated typefaces to fit the new set theme (elegant, space-like aesthetics). Ensured body font was a serif typeface to ensure better readability.

02

Upgrade the community sign-up flow

I proposed making a change to the community leaderboards where instead of picking Twitch emotes, participants could pick a TFT tactician/little legend to use as their icon. This was approved and I designed an icon selector for the sign-up page.

Created a confirmation/error page to let participants know if their sign up was successful. Incorporated set-related assets (art of each individual god) to the pages depending on if sign up was successful or if an error occured.

Development

This was my very first experience using the Angular framework when developing front-end. I had previous experience using React and React Native so I wasn’t entirely lost but there were still a decent amount of things I had to learn and become accustomed to. I learned as I worked and spent about five weeks making the front-end.

FINAL PRODUCT

Launching to the public...

The updated website for the Set 17 BoxBox Bootcamp launched on April 3, 2026. BoxBox featured it on stream (clip below, timestamp from 17:40 - 19:15).

Success...?

The overall reception of the new website was very positive.

However, there was one major issue... Mobile view of the leaderboards did not work, at all. This issue was flagged but the team decided that it was the of lowest priority to fix compared to daily operations (answering tickets, fixing functional bugs, etc.), since it was a visual bug and only affected mobile users.

The Numbers

Over the course of Bootcamp, the website amassed a total of 120.44k unique visitors, with a peak of 13.45k unique visitors. This data highlights that about 30% of users were accessing the website on mobile/tablet devices - this percentage was much higher than what the team previously believed, which was around 5-10% of users.

LEARNINGS

What did I learn from working on this project?

01

Avoid assuming the hardware demographics of users

The team and I did not have previous data regarding the device/hardware demographics of users and incorrectly assumed that very few users were accessing the website on mobile/tablet devices. Now that we have data regarding device demographics, the team and I have raised the priority of providing mobile support.

02

Learn how to design with a mobile-first attitude in mind

While I have designed mobile apps and web apps in the past, I have never designed a platform that required equal support on desktop and mobile. I was able to adjust most of the pages on the website to accommodate mobile viewports by adding responsive CSS styling but some of these adjustments do not look mobile-native. Designing for more than one viewport is something I will incorporate into my workflow (where appropriate) moving forward.
